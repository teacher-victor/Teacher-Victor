OpinionThe Meridian Review · World Affairs18 November 2026

By our foreign affairs columnist, a former delegate to the UN in New York

On 7 April 2026, eleven of the fifteen members of the Security Council voted for a resolution on shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, one of the busiest oil routes on the planet. The text had already been watered down to avoid trouble: an earlier draft would have allowed states to take forceful defensive measures, whereas the final version merely encouraged them to coordinate their efforts. Even so, it failed. Russia and China voted no, Colombia and Pakistan abstained, and because the two no votes came from permanent members, a majority of eleven counted for nothing. That is the veto in a single afternoon.

Scenes like this explain why so many people now ask what the United Nations is actually for, and whether multilateralism, the idea that countries should solve shared problems together, still works. Although the organisation turned eighty last year, it can feel less like a parliament of the world than a waiting room. The question deserves a serious answer, not a slogan. Mine is this: the UN is weaker than its founders hoped, but it is still more useful than its critics admit, and the decisions taken in the coming months will show which of those two descriptions survives.

The case against

Start with the obvious problem. On paper, the Charter gives the Security Council primary responsibility for international peace and security. In practice, any one of five countries can stop it from acting at all. When the interests of a permanent member or its allies are involved, the result is gridlock. By October 2025, delegations in New York had counted twenty vetoes since 2022, seven of them on Gaza. Each one left a crisis without a Council response, despite large majorities in favour of acting.

Money is the second problem. In December 2025 the General Assembly approved a regular budget of $3.45 billion for 2026, about $270 million less than the year before. The cut came with the loss of roughly 2,900 posts, close to a fifth of the staff paid for by that budget. Part of the reason was simple: members had not paid what they owed. At the start of December 2025, unpaid contributions, or arrears, had reached almost $1.6 billion. An institution cannot plan a peace process when it does not know whether it can pay its own staff in March.

Third, there is the elephant in the room: legitimacy. The five permanent seats reflect the winners of 1945, whereas the world of 2026 includes India, Brazil, Nigeria and dozens of states that did not exist when the Charter was signed. Many governments pay lip service to reform every September, during the high-level week, and then let the talks drift for another year.

The case for

Admittedly, that is a grim list. That said, it describes only one part of the organisation. Most of what the UN system does never reaches the Security Council chamber: vaccines delivered, refugees registered, elections observed, ships and aircraft that follow common rules. This work is dull, which is exactly why it succeeds. Even rivals who cannot agree on a war usually agree on the rules for international post and air traffic.

The General Assembly has also found ways to push back. In April 2022 it adopted resolution 76/262, known as the veto initiative, by consensus. Since then, whenever a permanent member casts a veto, the Assembly must convene within ten working days to debate it, and the country that used the veto is invited to speak first. A debate cannot overturn a veto. Even so, it makes the veto more expensive: a government that blocks a resolution now has to defend that choice in front of 193 members, not fifteen. That is how power becomes more accountable: slowly, publicly and imperfectly.

The Assembly matters in another way this year. António Guterres's second term ends on 31 December 2026, and the Security Council has been choosing his successor through a series of secret straw polls, in which each member marks every candidate as "encourage", "discourage" or "no opinion". A winner needs at least nine votes and no veto. Candidates have also answered questions from states and civil society at public town halls. In eighty years the UN has never had a woman as Secretary-General, whereas several of this year's strongest candidates are women. The process is still far from democratic, although it is more open than it has ever been.

"A debate cannot overturn a veto. Even so, it makes the veto more expensive."

What reform could look like

Two debates will shape the next decade. The first is the UN80 initiative, launched by the Secretary-General in March 2025 to merge overlapping offices, review thousands of mandates and cut costs. Critics say it is a budget exercise dressed up as reform. By contrast, its supporters argue that an organisation which cannot pay its bills cannot defend its principles either.

The second is an overhaul of the Security Council itself. Negotiations have been running since 2009 without a single agreed text. The G4 (Brazil, Germany, India and Japan) have proposed a Council of 25 or 26 members with new permanent seats. To make the idea easier to accept, they have suggested that new permanent members would not use the veto during a fifteen-year review period. African states, meanwhile, want at least two permanent seats for their continent. Every proposal has opponents, and any change to the Charter needs the approval of two-thirds of the membership, including all five permanent members. Reform, in other words, needs the permission of the very countries it would weaken.

So what is it for?

Here is my answer. The UN is not a world government, and it was never designed to be one. It is a place where states that distrust each other still have to meet, explain themselves and occasionally agree. Small countries, which have little power elsewhere, get a seat at the table and, in the Assembly, a vote that counts exactly as much as a superpower's. Despite its failures, no other forum offers that.

The organisation is at a crossroads. If the vetoes continue, the budget keeps shrinking and Council reform stays frozen, the UN will not collapse; it will simply become irrelevant, a building where speeches are given and nothing follows. If the veto initiative becomes harder to ignore, if members pay what they owe and if the next Secretary-General is chosen in the open, it can recover some of the authority it has lost.

Although it is fashionable to call the UN useless, the honest conclusion is less dramatic. It is an imperfect tool, but it is the only one built for all 193 countries at once. The real question for this year is not whether we need it. It is whether its members are willing to pay for it, in money and in political courage.

Vocabulary 14

multilateralism
noun

The idea that countries should solve shared problems together, through common rules and institutions.

The minister called the summit a victory for multilateralism.

abstained
verb, past

Chose not to vote for or against a proposal.

Three delegations abstained on the amendment.

permanent members
noun, plural

The five states (China, France, Russia, the UK and the US) that always sit on the Security Council and can use the veto.

None of the permanent members supported the new wording.

veto
noun

The right of a permanent member to block a Security Council decision by voting against it.

The draft failed because of a single veto.

gridlock
noun

A situation in which no progress is possible because the sides block each other.

Years of gridlock have left the reform talks without a text.

arrears
noun, plural

Money that is owed and should already have been paid.

Several states are in arrears on their contributions.

legitimacy
noun

The quality of being accepted as fair, lawful and deserving of authority.

Critics question the legitimacy of a council chosen in 1945.

consensus
noun

General agreement, reached without a formal vote.

The text was adopted by consensus.

convene
verb

To come together for a formal meeting, or to call such a meeting.

The committee will convene again on Thursday.

accountable
adjective

Required to explain and justify your actions to others.

Envoys must be accountable to the governments that send them.

straw polls
noun, plural

Informal, unofficial votes used to test how much support each option has.

The first straw polls showed no clear favourite.

mandates
noun, plural

Official instructions that give a body the authority and task to do something.

The review covers more than three thousand mandates.

overhaul
noun

A complete review and reform of a system.

The party promised an overhaul of the electoral law.

irrelevant
adjective

Not important or useful any longer; having no real effect.

Without funding, the agency risks becoming irrelevant.

Idioms & Set Phrases 6

watered down
phrasal verb (passive)

Made weaker or less strong, usually to win more support.

The final statement was watered down to keep everyone on board.

on paper
set phrase

In theory, or according to official documents, but not necessarily in practice.

On paper, the ceasefire is still in force.

the elephant in the room
idiom

An obvious, important problem that people avoid talking about.

Funding was the elephant in the room at every meeting.

lip service
idiom

Support that is expressed in words but not shown in action.

Many governments pay lip service to gender parity.

a seat at the table
idiom

A real chance to take part in an important discussion or decision.

Smaller states want a seat at the table when the rules are written.

at a crossroads
idiom

At a point where an important decision about the future must be made.

The alliance is at a crossroads after the election.

Useful Expressions (concession and contrast) 7

although
conjunction (concession)

Introduces a fact that contrasts with the main clause: although + subject + verb.

Although the vote failed, the debate changed minds.

whereas
conjunction (contrast)

Compares two facts that are different from each other.

The old draft allowed force, whereas the new one does not.

even so
linking adverb (concession)

Despite what was just said; starts a new sentence.

The text was weak. Even so, it was vetoed.

despite
preposition (concession)

Introduces a contrasting fact: despite + noun or -ing.

Despite wide support, the resolution failed.

admittedly
adverb (concession)

Accepts that a point against your argument is true.

Admittedly, the process is slow.

that said
linking phrase (contrast)

Introduces a point that balances or limits the one before it.

The budget is smaller. That said, priorities are clearer.

by contrast
linking phrase (contrast)

Shows a strong difference between two people, ideas or situations.

By contrast, the Assembly has 193 members.

Grammar

Concession and Contrast

Why it matters: in diplomacy you rarely win by ignoring the other side. A strong statement first accepts what is true in the opposing view, then explains why your position still holds. Concession makes you sound fair, and fairness makes you persuasive. Contrast lets you put two facts side by side so the listener sees the difference for themselves.

Common mistakes: despite never takes of (not "despite of") and never takes a full clause (not "despite the draft failed"). Use despite the fact that if you need a clause. Although and but do not go together in the same sentence.
Why op-eds use it: an op-ed is an argument, and readers trust a writer who shows the other side honestly. The classic op-ed move is "Yes, X is true. Even so, Y." This column follows that shape on a large scale: a full section of the case against the UN, then "Admittedly... That said..." to turn the argument round.
