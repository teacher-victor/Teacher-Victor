In public, governments in a crisis often insist that they are not talking to the other side. In private, they frequently are. A backchannel is a line of communication that runs outside official diplomacy: no press conference, no flags on the table, and sometimes no written record. This explainer looks at how these channels work, why leaders rely on them, and what three well-documented cases suggest about when they succeed.

Why not simply talk in public?

Official negotiations are a kind of theatre. Every statement is read by voters, allies and rivals, so delegations tend to repeat fixed positions and avoid any concession that might look like weakness. When public talks become deadlocked, a quiet channel lets both sides test the waters: they can float an idea, see how the other side reacts, and withdraw it if necessary.

The key advantage is deniability. If the contact fails or is exposed, each government can plausibly say that nothing official ever took place. That helps leaders save face at home, especially in front of hardliners who oppose any contact at all.

The go-between

Most backchannels depend on a third party. An intermediary may be a neutral government, a religious institution, a business figure or even an academic. Analysts usually distinguish between a mediator, who proposes solutions, and a facilitator, who simply provides a safe place, carries messages and keeps the conversation alive. Small and middle-sized states are widely believed to be particularly effective in this role, because both sides trust them and they are not seen as having leverage of their own to protect.

The choice of venue matters as well. Hotels in third countries, private homes and research institutes all offer something an embassy cannot: a place where delegations can arrive separately, without cameras, and where a meeting can be explained as something else if anyone asks. The intermediary's own discretion is part of the offer. A host who talks to journalists, or who tries to claim credit too early, is unlikely to be trusted with a second meeting.

Case 1

Beijing, 1971

By 1971 the United States and the People's Republic of China had had almost no direct contact for more than two decades. President Nixon set up a secret channel to the Chinese leadership through Pakistan's president, Yahya Khan, which also allowed the White House to bypass its own State Department. In July, national security adviser Henry Kissinger travelled to Pakistan on an official visit and then disappeared. The cover story was that he was ill and resting at a mountain resort. In fact, he had flown to Beijing as a secret envoy for two days of talks. The Chinese premier is said to have called the visit the best-kept secret since D-Day. Nixon revealed the trip only afterwards, in a televised speech, and travelled to China himself the following year.

Case 2

Oslo, 1993

In the early 1990s, official talks between Israeli and Palestinian delegations in Washington had stalled. In January 1993 a second, secret track opened in Norway. By the Norwegian foreign ministry's own account, Norway acted as a facilitator rather than a mediator, and the channel was organised with the help of a research institute, which gave the meetings an academic appearance. Negotiators met behind closed doors for months. The resulting Declaration of Principles was initialled at a secret ceremony in Oslo in August 1993, during an official visit that was, on paper, about something else entirely. It was signed publicly in Washington the following month.

Case 3

Havana and Washington, 2014

For more than fifty years the United States and Cuba had no diplomatic relations. Reportedly, the first face-to-face talks took place in Canada in June 2013 and continued for about eighteen months. Canadian officials hosted the meetings but, according to officials quoted at the time, did not take part in the substance. Pope Francis wrote to both presidents, and the Vatican hosted a meeting in the autumn of 2014. No session was held in either Cuba or the United States. When the two presidents announced the rapprochement on 17 December 2014, the news appears to have surprised almost everyone outside a very small circle.

The risks

Secrecy has a price. Because so few people are involved, experts inside the government may be excluded, and mistakes can go unnoticed. A leak can destroy trust in an afternoon, and an agreement negotiated in private may be rejected when it finally becomes public. Critics also argue that backchannels weaken accountability: parliaments and citizens learn about major decisions only after they have been made. In several cases the secret deal was allegedly easier to sign than to implement, because the people expected to carry it out had never been consulted.

What to watch

Backchannels have not disappeared in the age of social media. If anything, they seem to matter more when leaders attack each other online every day. In March 2026, for example, news reports described backchannel conversations between Washington and Tehran at a moment when public relations were openly hostile.

For anyone working in international affairs, the practical lesson is simple. When two governments say that they are not talking, it is worth asking three questions. Who could be carrying messages between them? What would each side need in order to compromise? And how could off the record contact later become a confidential draft and, eventually, a public agreement?

Vocabulary

The Language of Quiet Diplomacy

Every highlighted term from the explainer. The third group is the grammar focus of this lesson: language that lets you report a claim without owning it.

Vocabulary 12

backchannel
noun

A secret, unofficial line of communication between governments.

The ceasefire was first discussed through a backchannel in a neutral capital.

concession
noun

Something you give up or allow in order to reach an agreement.

Neither delegation was willing to make the first concession.

deadlocked
adjective

Unable to make progress because neither side will change its position.

The trade talks have been deadlocked since March.

deniability
noun

The ability to say, believably, that you did not know about or take part in something.

Using a private envoy gave the president deniability.

hardliners
noun, plural

People in a government or party who refuse to compromise.

Hardliners in parliament attacked the minister for meeting the other side.

intermediary
noun

A person or state that carries messages between two sides that will not talk directly.

A neutral embassy acted as intermediary between the two capitals.

facilitator
noun

A third party that hosts and supports talks without proposing solutions.

The host country described itself as a facilitator, not a mediator.

leverage
noun

Power to influence a situation or to pressure another side.

Sanctions gave the bloc considerable leverage in the negotiations.

envoy
noun

A person sent by a government to represent it in talks.

The prime minister sent a special envoy to the region.

rapprochement
noun

The return of friendly relations between two countries after a period of hostility.

The visit was seen as the first step towards rapprochement.

leak
noun

Secret information that reaches the press or the public.

A leak to a newspaper ended the talks within hours.

confidential
adjective

Meant to be kept secret or shared with only a few people.

The two sides exchanged a confidential draft.

Idioms & Set Phrases 5

test the waters
idiom

To find out how people will react before you commit yourself to something.

The minister used a private dinner to test the waters on a possible summit.

save face
idiom

To avoid looking weak or embarrassed in public.

The wording allowed both leaders to save face at home.

cover story
set phrase

A false explanation given to hide what is really happening.

The cover story was a routine visit to a trade fair.

behind closed doors
idiom

In private, without the public or the press present.

The final text was agreed behind closed doors.

off the record
set phrase

Not official, and not to be quoted or reported.

The ambassador spoke off the record to two journalists.

Useful Expressions (hedging and reporting) 7

widely believed to
hedging phrase

Many people think this is true, but the writer does not claim it as a proven fact.

Neutral states are widely believed to make the best hosts.

is said to
reporting passive

People say this; the writer cannot confirm it personally.

The envoy is said to have met his counterpart twice.

Reportedly
hedging adverb

According to reports; signals that the information comes from others.

Reportedly, the two delegations stayed in the same hotel.

according to
reporting phrase

Names the source of a claim, so that the writer is not responsible for it.

According to officials, no agreement has been signed.

appears to
hedging verb

Seems true from the evidence available, but is not certain.

The announcement appears to have surprised the opposition.

allegedly
hedging adverb

Claimed by someone, but not proven.

The document was allegedly drafted in a single night.

seem to
hedging verb

Give the impression of being true; a cautious way to state a trend.

Quiet channels seem to work best when both leaders need a result.

Grammar

Hedging and Distancing

Why it matters: in diplomacy and policy analysis, much of what you write about cannot be fully verified: secret meetings, private intentions, anonymous sources. Hedging lets you pass on information accurately while showing how sure you are. Stating a rumour as a fact can damage your credibility, or your country's position.

1. Direct claim or hedged claim?

Direct claimHedged claim
ExampleThe premier called it the best-kept secret since D-Day.The premier is said to have called it the best-kept secret since D-Day.

RiskIf it is wrong, the writer is wrong.If it is wrong, the source is wrong.
Use it forDocumented, public factsReports, estimates, motives, secret events

Why explainers use it: an analyst writing about secret diplomacy is almost never an eyewitness. The genre mixes public record (dates, signed documents) with reported detail (who said what in a closed room). Hedging marks the border between the two, so the reader always knows which is which.
